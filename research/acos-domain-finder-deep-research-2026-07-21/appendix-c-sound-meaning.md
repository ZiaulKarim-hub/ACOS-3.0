## Q1. Sound symbolism / phonaesthetics for "strong" and "pleasant" names

### 1a. Consonants: plosives vs sonorants vs fricatives

**Claim: Plosives/stops (p, b, t, d, k, g) are overrepresented in top brand names and associated with strength, energy, memorability.** — **Probable-to-Verified** (one corpus study + one experimental follow-up + convergent memory literature cited within them; corpus study is single-sample, English-market-specific)

- Pogacar, Plant, Rosulek & Kouril (2015), "Sounds good: Phonetic sound patterns in top brand names," *Marketing Letters* 26:549–563 [Tier 1]. Phonetically transcribed the Interbrand Top-100 brand names vs. a benchmark random sample of general brand names. Findings (from full text, extracted from the PDF at https://static1.squarespace.com/static/586d3b8c579fb3b92bfadb89/t/58725a088419c2902d0983f7/1483889162049/Sounds+good_Phonetic+sound+patterns+in+top+brand+names+2015.pdf):
  - **Every plosive (p, b, t, d, k, g) is overrepresented among top brands** (p: p<.03; b, t, d, k: p<.001; g: p<.01).
  - In word-initial position, 4 of 6 plosives are significantly overrepresented.
  - **Critical caveat the rubric must carry:** when non-English-origin brand names are included, the word-initial plosive pattern *reverses* (only "b" and "k" differ, and they are UNDERrepresented) — "the pattern of differences in this sound category is language specific."
  - Also overrepresented: fricatives **v, s, z, h** (s contrary to older Schloss 1981/Vanden Bergh 1990 letter-based studies), nasal **m**, approximant **l**. Underrepresented: **th, n, r, w, "dj"**, and the back vowel **"u"** (as in Budweiser).
  - This is **correlational** — the authors explicitly say overrepresented sounds "should be tested experimentally."
- Pogacar, Kouril, Carpenter & Kellaris (2018), "Implicit and explicit preferences for brand name sounds," *Marketing Letters* 29:241–259, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11002-018-9456-7 [Tier 1]. Experimental follow-up: people implicitly AND explicitly prefer the sounds common in top brand names (S, M, L, long-E), and implicit preference correlates with willingness to pay. Process evidence pointed to phonetic-frequency preference, ruling out pronunciation-fluency and familiarity accounts for this specific effect.
- Word-initial plosives improve brand-name recognition and recall (Vanden Bergh et al. 1987; Cortese 1998; Lowrey, Shrum & Dubitsky 2003, *Journal of Advertising* — cited within Pogacar 2015) — **Probable** (cited secondhand; convergent).
- Industry corroboration [Tier 2]: Lexicon Branding (BlackBerry, Swiffer, Pentium, Dasani) has worked with Stanford linguists (William Leben) on sound symbolism; founder David Placek: "Research shows 'B' is perceived to be one of the most reliable sounds in the English language" — the double-plosive B…B in BlackBerry was deliberate. https://www.forbes.com/sites/charlesrtaylor/2023/06/13/developing-effective-brand-names-lessons-from-a-naming-guru/ ; https://wtop.com/business-finance/2019/09/swiffer-blackberry-dasani-meet-the-man-who-named-your-favorite-products/

**Claim: Voiced vs voiceless and stops vs fricatives carry gender/strength connotations — but the literature contains a genuine internal contradiction.** — **Verified that effects exist; direction partially contested**

- Klink (2000), "Creating Brand Names With Meaning: The Use of Sound Symbolism," *Marketing Letters* 11(1):5–20, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1008184423824 [Tier 1]: voiceless consonants (p, t, k, f, s) perceived as smaller, **less potent**, lighter, sharper; **voiced consonants (b, d, g, v, z) perceived as more potent and more masculine** (also "creamier"). Contrary to his own predictions, words with initial stops were rated *more feminine* than fricative-initial words.
- Guèvremont & Grohmann (2015), "Consonants in brand names influence brand gender perceptions," *European Journal of Marketing* 49(1/2):101–122, https://www.emerald.com/ejm/article-abstract/49/1-2/101/88034 [Tier 1]: **stops enhance brand masculinity; fricatives enhance brand femininity** — note this is the opposite direction from Klink's stop-femininity anomaly. Flag for the rubric: use *voicing* (well-replicated potency cue) rather than stop-vs-fricative gender claims.
- Pathak et al. (2020), "Harsh voices, sound branding: How voiced consonants in a brand's name can alter its perceived attributes," *Psychology & Marketing*, https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mar.21346 [Tier 1] — further support that voiced consonants shift perceived attributes toward harsher/more rugged.

**Implementable rule (strength):** for "sounds strong," reward ≥1 plosive, especially word-initial and especially voiced (b, d, g); sonorants (l, m, n) + s contribute smoothness/likability (l, m, s overrepresented in top brands and preferred in Pogacar 2018). "Strong AND nice to hear" = plosive spine + sonorant/vowel flesh (the BlackBerry, Google, Tesla pattern).

### 1b. Bouba/kiki (rounded vs spiky sound mapping)

- Ramachandran & Hubbard (2001), "Synaesthesia — a window into perception, thought and language," *J. Consciousness Studies* 8(12):3–34, https://www.sfu.ca/~kathleea/colour/docs/Ram&Hub_2001.pdf [Tier 1]: **95–98%** of US college students and Tamil speakers matched "bouba" to the round shape, "kiki" to the spiky one. Maurer et al. (2006) replicated with children. — **Verified**
- Ćwiek et al. (2022), "The bouba/kiki effect is robust across cultures and writing systems," *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B* 377:20200390, https://doi.org/10.1098/RSTB.2020.0390 (full text https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8591387/) [Tier 1]: across **25 languages, 9 families, 10 scripts**: overall **72% congruent responses (95% CrI 56–82%)**; effect reliable in **17 of 25 languages**; at/below chance in **Romanian, Mandarin, Turkish** (Romanian only 36%); "bouba" side of the effect stronger than "kiki" (logit +0.79). Mechanism: continuous voicing + rounded vowels + labials → "round"; **voiceless stops (k) + spectral discontinuities → "spiky."** — **Verified, including its limits: NOT universal, weaker in some major markets.**
- Rubric use: this is the deep mechanism licensing "plosive = hard/strong, sonorant = soft/smooth" scoring, with a cross-language robustness of roughly 72%, not 100%.

### 1c. Vowel sound symbolism (front = small/fast/light; back = big/heavy/strong)

**Verified** — the single best-replicated finding in brand phonetics:

- Klink (2000) [Tier 1]: front-vowel names (e.g., "nidax") rated smaller, lighter, milder, thinner, softer, faster, colder, more bitter, **more feminine, weaker**, prettier than back-vowel names ("nodax") across paired comparisons. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1008184423824
- Yorkston & Menon (2004), "A Sound Idea: Phonetic Effects of Brand Names on Consumer Judgments," *JCR* 31(1):43–51, https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/31/1/43/1812051 (PDF: https://web.stanford.edu/class/linguist62n/yorkston.pdf) [Tier 1]: ice cream named **"Frosh"** (back vowel) judged smoother, creamier, richer than **"Frish"**; effect is automatic and occurs even under cognitive load, but attenuates when consumers are told the name is randomly generated.
- Lowrey & Shrum (2007), "Phonetic Symbolism and Brand Name Preference," *JCR* 34(3):406–414, https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/34/3/406/1798924 [Tier 1]: **effect size ≈ 2:1 preference margins** — front-vowel names ("brimley") preferred ~2:1 for convertibles and knives (small/sharp/fast desirable); back-vowel names ("bromley") preferred ~2:1 for SUVs and hammers (big/heavy/strong desirable). **Key implementable insight: vowel "strength" is category-congruence, not an absolute.** ScienceDaily summary: https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/09/070912130815.htm
- Pogacar (2015) corpus [Tier 1]: most-common/most-overrepresented vowel in top brands = "e" as in eBay (long E); also the a's of AOL/Kraft/Starbucks; the "o" of "posh" is the only overrepresented back vowel (consistent with Lowrey & Shrum's finding that this /ɑ/-type sound is positively perceived enough to "trump" category incongruence); "u" (Budweiser) underrepresented.
- Cross-language extension: Shrum, Lowrey, Luna, Lerman & Liu (2012), "Sound symbolism effects across languages: Implications for global brand names," *IJRM*, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167811612000420 [Tier 1] — front/back vowel effects replicated for French, Spanish, Chinese speakers — **Probable** (abstract-level verification only).

### 1d. Syllable count, stress, vowel endings

**Claim: 2–3 syllables is optimal.** — **Probable as a descriptive norm; the causal "two-syllable rule" is NOT well-evidenced.**

- "Rightsizing brand names: the effects of brand name length on brand name attitude and recognition," *Journal of Brand Management* (2026), https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41262-026-00436-0 + VUB/SMIT policy brief https://smit.research.vub.be/en/policy-brief-94 [Tier 1/2]: the textbook two-syllable rule has "surprisingly limited" empirical basis; **recognition accuracy actually rises with length** (longer = more distinctive); attitude shows an **inverted-U when length is measured in phonemes** (not syllables). Implication: score fluency/pronounceability, not raw syllable count; treat 2–3 syllables as a soft default, not a hard rule.
- Descriptive stat [Tier 3]: world's top-100 brands average **6.4 characters, 2.1 syllables**; 1–3 syllables is the observed sweet spot. https://www.frozenlemons.com/blog/how-long-should-business-name-be
- Counterpoint [Tier 1]: Pathak (2019), *Psychology & Marketing*, longer names can *enhance luxury* perception, https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mar.21247 — length interacts with positioning.

**Claim: initial stress (trochaic) is preferred/easier for English audiences.** — **Verified in psycholinguistics; brand-specific effect is inference (Open).**

- **94% of English disyllabic words are trochaic** (initial stress), and English speakers default nonwords to initial stress; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4666842/ [Tier 1].
- Trochaic bias overrides stress typicality in English lexical development, *J. Child Language*, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/trochaic-bias-overrides-stress-typicality-in-english-lexical-development/F27C3F055C36875399110BAAA41BEBAE [Tier 1].
- Recognition-memory advantage for trochaic items (Spanish cognate study), *Memory & Cognition*, https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-013-0305-x [Tier 1].
- No direct study of brand recall × stress found → judge should treat initial stress as a fluency bonus, not a requirement. **Open** at brand level.

**Claim: names ending in vowels (-a, -o) are more likable.** — **Open / partially wrong as stated. The real finding is gender-marking + congruence:**

- Yorkston & de Mello (2005), "Linguistic Gender Marking and Categorization," *JCR* 32(2):224–234, https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/32/2/224/1799092 [Tier 1]: **-a codes feminine, -o masculine** (Spanish formal gender); congruent marking (masculine "Aizo" for beer) improved recall and evaluation vs incongruent ("Aiza"). So a vowel ending helps only when its gender connotation matches the brand's positioning. No evidence for a general likability bonus for vowel endings — do not encode one.

### 1e. Processing fluency (pronounceability → liking/trust) and its limits

- Alter & Oppenheimer (2006), "Predicting short-term stock fluctuations by using processing fluency," *PNAS* 103(24):9369–9372, https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0601071103 [Tier 1]: pronounceable-name IPOs outperformed unpronounceable ones — $1,000 basket difference of **+$112 after 1 day**; pronounceable-ticker basket +$85.35 (1 day), +$20.25 (1 year); effect decays with time. — **Verified as published; short-term, market-context.**
- Song & Schwarz (2009), "If It's Difficult to Pronounce, It Must Be Risky," *Psychological Science* 20(2):135–138, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02267.x (PDF: https://dornsife.usc.edu/norbert-schwarz/wp-content/uploads/sites/231/2023/11/09_ps_song___schwarz_fluency___risk.pdf) [Tier 1]: hard-to-pronounce food additives ("Hnegripitrom") rated more harmful than easy ones ("Magnalroxate"); hard-to-pronounce amusement rides rated more sickening AND more exciting.
- **LIMIT (must be in the rubric's epistemics):** Bahník & Vranka (2017), "If It's Difficult to Pronounce, It Might Not Be Risky," *Psychological Science* 28(4):427–436, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797616685770 [Tier 1]: replicated with the original 10 stimuli, but **zero effect across 50 newly generated stimuli** — the fluency-risk link does not generalize to new names. Treat fluency as a *liking* heuristic, not a precise risk predictor.
- Laham, Koval & Alter (2012), "The name-pronunciation effect," *JESP* 48:752–756, https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~aalter/namefluency.pdf [Tier 1]: easy-to-pronounce surnames judged more positively; lawyers with more pronounceable names occupy higher firm positions; effect independent of length, foreignness, unusualness. — Fluency→liking overall: **Verified (direction), with generalization caveats.**

## Q2. The meaning spectrum (R2) — trademark distinctiveness

### The Abercrombie spectrum — **Verified** (primary case + USPTO-aligned Tier 2 legal sources)

*Abercrombie & Fitch Co. v. Hunting World, Inc.*, 537 F.2d 4 (2d Cir. 1976) (Friendly, J.) established the five-category spectrum; case record: https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/judgments/details/925 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abercrombie_%26_Fitch_Co._v._Hunting_World,_Inc. [Tier 1 primary legal]. Categories, ordered weakest→strongest, with examples (BitLaw https://www.bitlaw.com/trademark/degrees.html ; INTA https://www.inta.org/fact-sheets/trademark-strength/ ; https://blog.trademarkelite.com/post/2026/05/01/strong-vs-weak-trademarks-what-the-uspto-looks-for ; https://stemerlaw.com/2025/04/02/... [Tier 2]):

| Category | Definition | Examples | Legal strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Generic** | The thing itself | "Smartphone," "E-mail," "Safari" (for safari gear — the actual Abercrombie holding) | **No protection ever** — veto |
| **Descriptive** | Directly describes product/feature | FAST BAUD (modems), LIGHT (laptops), American Airlines-type names | Protectable **only after acquired secondary meaning** |
| **Suggestive** | "Requires imagination, thought and perception to reach a conclusion as to the nature of goods" (Abercrombie) | **Netflix** (net + flicks), **Pinterest** (pin + interest), Microsoft, Netscape, Coppertone, Airbus, Greyhound | **Inherently distinctive — protectable immediately, no secondary meaning needed** |
| **Arbitrary** | Real word, unrelated to product | **Apple** (computers), Amazon (retail), Dove (soap/chocolate), Lotus, Sun | Strong |
| **Fanciful** | Coined, no prior meaning | **Kodak, Xerox**, Exxon, Google, Rolex, Pentium | Strongest |

**Key point VERIFIED (the R2 premise):** Suggestive marks are exactly the "cleverly alludes to the function" class, and they sit on the *inherently distinctive* side of the line — "Devices that are fanciful, arbitrary, or suggestive are considered distinctive enough to function as trademarks" (BitLaw), whereas descriptive marks need years of secondary meaning. **So R2's "fallback" (clever allusion instead of literal real word) is legally the better outcome than a plainly descriptive real word.** A judge rubric should therefore score descriptive real words BELOW suggestive coinages/allusions, and generic terms as an automatic 0/veto. Suggestive is also the marketing sweet spot: it communicates for free (R1 relevance) while staying protectable — the trade-off noted industry-wide is that fanciful names are strongest legally but cost the most to imbue with meaning.

### Naming-industry frameworks that grade semantic fit [Tier 2]

- **Igor Naming Guide** (Igor International, free ~100-page guide, 2022 ed.): four-category taxonomy — **Functional** (descriptive: Infoseek, LookSmart) → **Invented** (Agilent, Google-as-coinage) → **Experiential** (real-word direct experience: Netscape, Palm Pilot) → **Evocative** (positioning-evoking real words: Virgin, Apple, Oracle). Igor explicitly ranks Evocative highest for differentiation and Functional lowest ("lowest common denominator of names"). Competitive taxonomy charts position candidates against every competitor name in category. https://www.igorinternational.com/process/naming-company-names-taxonomy.php ; PDF: https://www.igorinternational.com/process/i/Igor%20Naming%20Guide%202022.pdf — Note Igor's axis is *experience/evocation*, roughly parallel to descriptive→suggestive→arbitrary, and it independently converges on "literal = weak, evocative = strong."
- **Lexicon Branding** (published positions via interviews): coined-but-allusive names built on sound symbolism (Swiffer from swipe/swish/swiff; Dasani engineered with soft vowels for purity; BlackBerry chosen because "berry" + plosive B's beat literal e-mail-device names). https://newsroom.ucla.edu/magazine/david-placek-lexicon-brand-naming ; https://www.forbes.com/sites/charlesrtaylor/2023/06/13/developing-effective-brand-names-lessons-from-a-naming-guru/
- **Interbrand**: no single public scoring rubric found for names specifically; its Top-100 valuation list is the standard corpus for "successful name" studies (Pogacar 2015). General industry criteria (distinctive / memorable / meaningful / pronounceable / scalable) recur across agency methodologies, e.g., https://www.ideagrove.com/blog/naming-agency-tips-for-effective-brand-naming ; https://martinroll.com/resources/articles/branding/brand-naming-techniques/ — **Probable** (convergent Tier 2, no canonical source).

## Q3. Proposed implementable 0/1/2 rubric (three judge dimensions)

**Design notes baked in:** (i) R1-relevance and legal/meaning strength pull in opposite directions at the arbitrary/fanciful end — an arbitrary name (Apple) is legally strong but fails this user's R1, so RELEVANCE is scored purely as the user requirement while MEANING QUALITY encodes the spectrum with a generic veto; a **suggestive** name is the only class that can score 2/2 on both, which matches R2's design. (ii) SOUND merges strength (plosive spine, voiced consonants, category-congruent vowels) with euphony/fluency (sonorants, easy pronunciation, initial stress, 2–3 syllable soft default).

| Score | (a) RELEVANCE to stated business (R1) | (b) MEANING QUALITY on the spectrum (R2) | (c) SOUND: strength + euphony (R3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0** | No discernible connection to the business's function, benefit, or feeling even after reflection; connection requires the founder's private backstory. *Examples: "Verizon" pitched for a bakery; "Maple" for a cybersecurity firm; random pleasant word.* | **Generic for the category = automatic veto** ("The Coffee Shop" for a coffee shop; "Smartphone"). Also 0: flatly descriptive strings with no transformation ("Fast Baud," "Speedy Delivery Co.," "Quality Plumbing Services") — legally weakest protectable class, needs secondary meaning. | Disfluent: hard consonant clusters for an English reader, ambiguous spelling→pronunciation, no natural stress point, or 5+ syllables of mush. *Examples: Xobni (failed "inbox"-backwards startup), "Hnegripitrom" (Song & Schwarz stimulus), unpronounceable initialisms like HSGWX.* |
| **1** | Connection exists but is oblique, category-level only, or needs one sentence of explanation to land. *Examples: Amazon for a bookstore (big-river = big-selection metaphor, needed explaining in 1995); Sonos for audio (Latin root, semi-opaque); a coffee brand named "Dawn."* | Real word used arbitrarily (Apple, Dove) — legally strong but semantically mute for this user's R2; OR competent fanciful coinage with word-like morphology (Kodak, Xerox, Zalto) — strongest legally, but not a "meaningful real word" and doesn't allude. Also 1: descriptive-plus-twist compounds that lean literal ("PayPal" borderline 1–2). | Pronounceable and inoffensive but flat: either all-soft (no plosive spine — pleasant but not "strong": Aviva, Sonos-class smoothness) or all-hard (plosive pile-up without sonorant/vowel relief: Grubbt, Kwikkt), or 4+ syllables that drag (Accenture-class). Unambiguous pronunciation required to reach 1. |
| **2** | Function/benefit/feeling of the described business is evident on first hearing, via direct semantic field OR a metaphor a stranger decodes unaided. *Examples: Instacart (grocery delivery), Netflix (films over the net), Coppertone (tanning), Greyhound (fast buses).* | **Suggestive sweet spot:** meaningful real word or transparent portmanteau/coinage that *alludes* to the function without describing it — inherently distinctive, protectable day one. *Examples: Netflix, Pinterest, Swiffer, Coppertone, Slack (real word, allusive for messaging), BlackBerry.* Real-word-when-possible satisfied; clever allusion equally acceptable (and legally equal). | 2–3 syllables (soft rule; phoneme-count inverted-U), initial stress, ≥1 plosive — ideally word-initial, voiced for potency — balanced against sonorants (l, m, n) / s / open vowels; vowel symbolism congruent with the business's key attribute (back vowels o/a for big-heavy-rugged, front vowels i/e for fast-light-precise); spelling reads one way. *Examples: BlackBerry, Google, Tesla, Kodak, Target, Pepsi.* |

**Additional judge rules:**
- **Generic veto** (dimension b, score 0) is absolute — no averaging can rescue it (no legal protection ever; Abercrombie).
- **Vowel-congruence rule** (dimension c): judge must first infer the business's dominant attribute (heavy/strong vs fast/light) from the user's description, then check vowel fit — this operationalizes Lowrey & Shrum's ~2:1 congruence preference and prevents naive "back vowels always = strong" scoring.
- **Gender-marking check**: a terminal -a reads feminine, terminal -o/consonant masculine; score down only on *incongruence* with positioning (Yorkston & de Mello 2005).

**Evidence-strength flags per rule:**

| Rule | Status |
|---|---|
| Front/back vowel symbolism + category congruence | **Well-evidenced** (Klink 2000; Yorkston & Menon 2004; Lowrey & Shrum 2007; cross-language extension 2012) |
| Bouba/kiki basis for plosive=hard / sonorant=soft | **Well-evidenced**, but ~72% cross-culturally, at-chance in Romanian/Mandarin/Turkish (Ćwiek 2022) |
| Fluency → liking | **Well-evidenced direction** (Alter & Oppenheimer 2006; Laham 2012), **generalization limited** (Bahník & Vranka 2017) — use as bonus, not fine-grained predictor |
| Pogacar phoneme table (plosives, l/m/s/z/v/h, long-E overrepresented) | **Single corpus study, correlational, English-market**; partially reinforced by one experimental follow-up (Pogacar 2018). Pattern reverses for non-English-origin names — do not hard-code |
| Voiced consonants = potency | **Probable** (Klink 2000; Pathak 2020) |
| Stops=masculine vs fricatives=feminine | **Contested** — Klink 2000 found the opposite for initial stops vs Guèvremont & Grohmann 2015; keep out of scoring |
| 2–3 syllables | **Descriptive norm only** (top-100 mean 2.1 syllables); causal two-syllable rule undermined by 2026 JBM study — score phoneme-length fluency instead |
| Initial stress preference | **Well-evidenced in psycholinguistics** (94% trochaic disyllables), **inference at brand level** |
| Suggestive > descriptive legally; generic veto | **Verified, primary legal authority** (Abercrombie 1976; BitLaw; INTA) |
| Vowel-ending likability | **Not supported** — only gender-congruence effects exist |

## Q4. Cross-language sound-level safety

**Industry practice ("linguistic disaster check") — Verified as standard practice [Tier 2, convergent across 6+ firms]:**

- **Catchword** (https://catchwordbranding.com/naming/linguistics-cultural-analysis/): panels of **3 native-speaker evaluators per language, living in-country, marketing-literate** (not mere translators); checks pronounceability, negative connotations, slang/regional meanings, competitor resemblance; run at the **shortlist stage (7–10 names)**, concurrent with legal screening, ~2 weeks; recommends at minimum French, Italian, German, Spanish + Mandarin, Cantonese, Japanese, Korean.
- Same offering pattern: Zinzin (https://www.zinzin.com/process/linguistic-screening/), Skriptor (https://www.skriptor.com/services/linguistic-screening), NameStormers (https://www.namestormers.com/capabilities/linguistic-screening/), Eurotranslate (https://eurotranslate.rs/translation-services/linguistic-brand-name-validation/) — consistently: **sound (not just spelling) similarity to offensive words, connotation, pronounceability, aesthetics** in each target language. Seven-step process context: https://howbrandsarebuilt.com/a-seven-step-process-for-brand-naming/
- No published, validated *algorithmic* method found — the industry gold standard is human native-speaker panels. **Open** whether LLM-based per-language screening matches panel quality (no benchmark exists).

**Verified cautionary cases (and one anti-case):**

- **REAL — Mitsubishi Pajero**: "pajero" = Spanish vulgar slang ("wanker"); renamed **Montero** in Spanish-speaking markets/US and **Shogun** in the UK. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_Pajero [Tier 3] — **Verified**
- **REAL (caught pre-launch) — Honda "Fitta"**: intended name is a vulgar Swedish/Norwegian word for vagina; renamed **Jazz** (Europe) / **Fit** (US/Japan). https://www.carscoops.com/2007/09/why-honda-didnt-call-fit-jazz-by-its/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honda_Fit [Tier 3] — **Probable-Verified**
- **MYTH — Chevy Nova** ("no va" = "doesn't go" killed Latin-American sales): debunked; the Nova sold fine, and "nova"/"no va" are not homophones in Spanish. https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/chevrolet-nova-name-spanish/ ; https://www.npr.org/2011/10/19/141473384/letters-the-myth-of-the-chevy-nova [Tier 3] — **Verified as myth.** Lesson for the rubric: screen on *phonetic* similarity judged by (native-speaker-equivalent) review, not on eyeballed letter-string coincidences.

**Implementable LLM-judge analog (flagged as heuristic, unvalidated):** at shortlist stage, for each candidate run a per-language check across a fixed roster (Spanish, French, German, Italian, Portuguese, Swedish/Norwegian, Dutch, Polish, Russian, Turkish, Arabic, Hindi/Urdu, Mandarin-pinyin, Japanese-romaji, Korean-romanized): (1) transliterate the name's *sound*, not spelling; (2) ask whether the whole name or any salient syllable is a homophone/near-homophone of a profane, scatological, sexual, or comic word; (3) fail only on native-plausible phonology (the Nova lesson — stress and syllable boundaries must actually match); (4) surface flags for human confirmation rather than auto-veto. This mirrors the Catchword scorecard structure but has no published validation — **Open**.
